From spatial deviations to typographic structure

Standard Error explores how the visual logic of liminal spaces can be translated into letterforms. A familiar typographic structure becomes a place to test repetition, displacement, distortion, uniformity, and recognition.

PROJECT OVERVIEW

THE TRANSLATION FROM RESEARCH TO TYPOGRAPHY

Five design experiments inspired by the research. These connections are my interpretation of spatial conditions through letterforms.

SPATIAL STARTING POINT TYPOGRAPHIC INTERPRETATION
Unexpected placement 01. DISPLACEMENT

Shift components and disrupt their expected spacing.
Repeated features 02. REPETITION

Duplicate letterform elements until familiarity becomes excess.
Altered sizes and proportions 03. DISTORTION

Deform familiar structures while retaining traces of their original form.
Expectations of human presence 04. UNIFORMITY

Explore an absence of human irregularity through mechanical consistency.

Alter the visual cues that make letters identifiable.

Research archive

The idea started from the uncanny valley phenomenon. Uncanny valley doesn’t just occur from faces. It also occurs from certain places, known as liminal spaces. Standard Error takes its starting point from Structural deviations drive an uncanny valley of physical places, a 2022 study by Alexander Diel and Michael Lewis. Their research investigates how disruptions to familiar architectural patterns can make physical environments feel uncanny. This project brings that question into typography. If we understand letters as structures—with recognizable proportions, repeated components, and expected relationships—what happens when those expectations are disrupted? Using Liberation Sans as a familiar starting point, Standard Error explores five variable axes: Displacement, Repetition, Distortion, Uniformity, and Recognition. Each axis translates a spatial idea into a typographic operation, allowing readers to move between familiar letterforms and increasingly unfamiliar configurations. These axes are experimental interpretations of the research. The typeface asks whether disrupting the structures we read can evoke something of the unease found in the spaces we inhabit.
